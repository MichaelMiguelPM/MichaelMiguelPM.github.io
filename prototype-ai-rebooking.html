1 · Discovery with AI

Read everything before deciding anything

The brief: as an AI PM at United, prototype an AI-driven rebooking experience for corporate travelers whose flights are cancelled about a week out. United wants to cut the share rebooked on other airlines from about 25% to 17%. I had 2.5 hours, a sample of one agent's morning queue, and a brief I couldn't open at first.

Confirm access first

I handed Claude every file (9 CSVs, a JSON export, the workbook and a Google Sheet) and asked it only to confirm what it could open. The Notion brief was behind a login, so I shared screenshots instead.

Frame who, what, how

Before any analysis I ranked what I needed to know: my role, United's goal, the agent's operating reality, then the data.

Let code read the data

Rather than eyeball 82 cases, I had Claude score all 344 rebooking options against each traveler's deadline, company policy and pre-approved budget.

Name the gaps

The data holds no record of what agents actually booked, so leakage can't be measured from it. Every idea had to become a hypothesis, not a claim.

2 · Learning the user's problem

United has the seats. Agents don't have the time.

The first surprise: in all 81 cases with options, at least one United flight was on time and within the pre-approved budget. With stricter checks for traveler preferences, red-eyes and connections it was still 74 of 81. Leakage wasn't a supply problem. It was a speed and trust problem.

The agent (primary user)

80 to 120 cancellations a day in about 3 hours. Under that pressure the first workable option wins, whatever airline it's on.

The traveler

Needs to arrive on time with their preferences respected. Half reply within 24 hours; held seats last only 24 to 48.

The travel manager

Owns the policy and budget. Some policies say "lowest fare, any carrier," which pulls bookings off United.

United

Wants travelers to stay on United. That's the conflict: it only works if United's option wins on merit, never by hiding a better one.

Profiles can't be trusted

36 of 81 travelers had stale, incomplete or conflicting profile data.

Seats are scarce

In 12 of 81 cases the best United option had 2 or fewer seats left.

Small fare gaps

In 9 cases another airline was cheaper, by $12 to $71, mostly for "any carrier" policies.

Missing deadlines

16 cases had no meeting time at all, and some sources contradicted each other.

The insight: steps 2 to 7 are reading and reasoning, which AI does fast. Steps 8 and 9 are waiting, which the product should run in parallel. The final choice and anything unclear stays with a person.

"Removing the agent from the happy path is the empathy. Their limited time goes to the cases that need a human decision."

The reframe that shaped the prototype

With strict rules (clean data, known deadline, an on-time United option within budget), 26 of 82 cases could run with no agent at all. That's roughly 50% more time per case for everything else. The agent stays accountable by setting the rules up front, reviewing a list of what the AI handled, and keeping an Undo.

5 · Picking hypotheses to test

From ten ideas to five worth building

I generated ten hypotheses for reaching 17%, checked each one against the data, then kept the five that got my attention and dropped or reshaped the rest.

  • One click on the top 1 to 3 United options. The in-policy pick comes first. Only 49 of 81 cases had three workable United options, so the list never pads with ones that don't work.
  • Hold the best seat the moment the cancellation arrives. This closes the gap between 24-hour replies and 24-to-48-hour holds. It's the one I'd test first.
  • Count Star Alliance partners United tickets as United options. They add backup seats when United's own flight is nearly full.
  • Flag small fare gaps instead of hiding them. I first thought United should cover extra hotel nights, but the data didn't support it. Instead, when another airline is under $50 cheaper and the policy allows any airline, the agent decides, with United preselected.
  • Show why, so agents trust the pick. Every recommendation cites the policy line, the deadline it protects and what it left out. Overrides are logged with a reason from a fixed list, which makes the 25% to 17% goal measurable.

AI decides

Within agency rules
  • Rank the queue
  • Read the disruption
  • Check data, policy and deadline
  • Pick and hold the top options

A person decides

Judgment and accountability
  • Traveler picks in one tap
  • Agent handles flagged cases

  • Agent can move off United, with a reason

How I'd know it works

Measured from day one
  • Top-pick acceptance rate
  • Holds converted vs. expired
  • Override and undo rate, by reason
  • Leakage: AI-handled vs. agent-handled

Not built, on purpose

Out of a 2.5-hour scope
  • Live seat inventory
  • Real email and approval routing
  • Parsing 180 policy PDFs
  • Flows 2 to 4 beyond one agent screen

The prototype

What you're looking at

A corporate traveler's morning nonstop is cancelled a week out, and a board meeting depends on that flight. Instead of a cancellation notice and a phone queue, the traveler gets one recommended flight that already fits their profile, their company's policy and their 3:00 PM deadline.

One-tap rebooking

The best in-policy flight on top, seat already held, with a plain note on the trade-off.

Why these options?

Shows the reasoning: the policy line it applied, the deadline it protected, and what it left out.

Policy-aware choices

Options over the $200 limit are routed to the travel manager for approval automatically.

Ask for something else

Earlier, later, nonstop, another airport or another day, then search again.

Graceful handoff

If nothing fits, a named agent takes over with full context. The seat stays held.

Exceptions-only agent view

Agents see only the cases that need them, plus what the AI handled, with Undo.

Use the Demo controls link in the top corner to jump between screens and switch scenarios. Concept work, not affiliated with any airline.
